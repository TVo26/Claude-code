# How Starlink Works: voiceover script (3:00)

The lines below are the on-screen captions, so reading them aloud keeps voice and picture in sync. Part 1 is a cold-open hook; its three lines start at 0 s, 5 s and 10 s. Every other part is 15 s with lines every 5 s (part 11 has four lines).

## Part 1 (0–15 s): Cold open
- Right now, thousands of satellites are racing over your head at 27,000 kilometres an hour.
- And this cabin has no cable and no phone line, yet it is online.
- How? The trick is simpler than you think, and I will show you in three minutes.

## Part 2 (15–30 s): Why old satellite internet was slow
- Satellite internet is not new, but it used to be painfully slow. Here is why.
- The satellites sit 36,000 kilometres up. Your request goes up and down, and so does the reply.
- That adds up to about 600 milliseconds of lag. Now watch what Starlink changes.

## Part 3 (30–45 s): The big idea: fly low
- Starlink's big idea? Fly much lower, only about 550 kilometres up.
- On the same scale, the old satellites are around 65 times higher.
- Shorter trip, faster reply. Simple. But there is a catch.

## Part 4 (45–60 s): The catch: they zoom
- The catch: down low, satellites race around at about 27,000 kilometres an hour.
- One flies across your whole sky in just a few minutes, then it is gone.
- So one satellite can never do the job alone. What is the fix?

## Part 5 (60–75 s): So launch thousands
- Send up thousands of them, in rings all around the planet.
- Rockets carry dozens at a time, and the boosters get reused.
- Now, wherever you stand, there is almost always one overhead.

## Part 6 (75–90 s): The clever flat dish
- But how does a flat dish keep up with all that motion? This is where it gets clever.
- Inside are lots of tiny antennas, all sending the same signal together.
- Shift each one's timing slightly, and the waves combine into a beam you can aim.

## Part 7 (90–105 s): Following a moving target
- Change the timing, and the beam re-aims in a fraction of a second.
- The dish tracks one satellite across the sky, then hands you to the next.
- This happens again and again, and you never notice.

## Part 8 (105–120 s): The journey of one click
- So what happens when you open a web page? Your request goes up to a satellite.
- It beams down to a ground station that is wired into the regular internet.
- Then the answer makes the same trip back, in just tens of milliseconds.

## Part 9 (120–135 s): Lasers in space
- Here is the part that blows my mind: the satellites also have lasers.
- Data can hop from one satellite to the next, like a relay race in space.
- That helps reach ships, planes and places far from any ground station.

## Part 10 (135–150 s): Why it feels fast
- So how much faster is it? Old satellite internet: roughly 600 milliseconds.
- Starlink: typically 25 to 60 milliseconds, not far from many home connections.
- That is enough for video calls and online games.

## Part 11 (150–165 s): The catches
- Now, it is not perfect. The dish needs a clear view of the sky.
- Heavy rain or snow can weaken the signal.
- Busy areas share limited capacity, so speeds can dip.
- And thousands of satellites worry astronomers and add to space junk.

## Part 12 (165–180 s): Recap
- So: fly low for speed, launch thousands, steer the beam with timing, link to the ground.
- That is how a cabin in the middle of nowhere gets internet from the sky.
- If that made you curious, subscribe, and tell me what to explain next.

## Fact notes for the narrator
- Distances, speeds and delays are rounded, typical figures (geostationary ≈ 36,000 km; Starlink ≈ 550 km; ≈ 27,000 km/h; old satellite lag ≈ 600 ms; Starlink typically 25–60 ms). Real results vary.
- Pictures are simplified: satellite sizes, orbit counts and the number of antennas in the dish are not to scale.
- The cabin story, the hook and the "Next: GPS" teaser are creative additions.
