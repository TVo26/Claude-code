# How Starlink Works: voiceover script (3:00)
Each part is 15 s. The lines below are the on-screen captions, so reading them aloud at a relaxed pace keeps voice and picture in sync.

## Part 1 (0–15 s): No cable, no problem
- Picture a cabin far from any town. No cable has ever been dug out here.
- Starlink's answer: skip the cable, and send the internet down from space.
- So how does it work? Let's follow one web page on its journey.

## Part 2 (15–30 s): The old way: far-off satellites
- Older internet satellites hover about 36,000 kilometres up, far above Earth.
- Light is fast, but a request goes up and down, and the reply goes up and down again.
- Add it up and you wait around 600 milliseconds. That is the lag you feel.

## Part 3 (30–45 s): The big idea: fly low
- Starlink flies much closer, only about 550 kilometres up.
- On the same scale, the old satellites are around 65 times higher.
- A shorter trip means a much faster reply.

## Part 4 (45–60 s): The catch: they zoom
- The catch? Down low, satellites race around Earth at about 27,000 kilometres an hour.
- From the ground, one flies across your whole sky in just a few minutes.
- So a single satellite can never do the job alone.

## Part 5 (60–75 s): So launch thousands
- So Starlink launches thousands of satellites, spread over many orbits around Earth.
- Rockets carry dozens at a time, and the boosters get reused.
- Wherever you stand, there is almost always one overhead to talk to.

## Part 6 (75–90 s): The clever flat dish
- At home you set up a flat dish. Unlike an old dish, it does not turn to follow satellites.
- Inside are lots of tiny antennas, all sending the same signal together.
- Fire them with tiny time offsets, and the waves add up into a beam pointing where you want.

## Part 7 (90–105 s): Following a moving target
- Because the beam is steered by timing, the dish can re-aim in a fraction of a second.
- It tracks one satellite across the sky, then switches to the next one coming up.
- These handoffs happen again and again, and you never notice.

## Part 8 (105–120 s): The journey of one click
- Say you open a web page. Your request goes from the dish up to a satellite.
- The satellite beams it down to a ground station, wired into the regular internet.
- The website's answer takes the same trip back, in just tens of milliseconds.

## Part 9 (120–135 s): Lasers in space
- Newer satellites also carry lasers that link them to each other.
- Data can hop from satellite to satellite, like a relay race in space.
- That helps reach ships, planes and remote places far from any ground station.

## Part 10 (135–150 s): Why it feels fast
- Compare the delay. Old satellite internet: roughly 600 milliseconds.
- Starlink is typically 25 to 60 milliseconds, not far from many home connections.
- That is fast enough for video calls and online games.

## Part 11 (150–165 s): The catches
- It is not perfect. The dish needs a clear view of the sky.
- Heavy rain or snow can weaken the signal.
- Busy areas share limited capacity, so speeds can dip.
- And thousands of satellites worry astronomers and add to space junk.

## Part 12 (165–180 s): Recap
- Fly low for speed. Launch thousands so one is always near.
- Steer the beam with timing, and link to ground stations on the ground.
- That is how a cabin in the middle of nowhere gets the internet from the sky.

## Fact notes for the narrator
- Distances, speeds and delays are rounded, typical figures (geostationary ≈ 36,000 km; Starlink ≈ 550 km; ≈ 27,000 km/h; old satellite lag ≈ 600 ms; Starlink typically 25–60 ms). Real results vary.
- Pictures are simplified: satellite sizes, orbit counts and the number of antennas in the dish are not to scale.
- The cabin story and the "Next up: GPS" teaser are creative additions.
