# How Starlink Works (multi-style): voiceover script, 2:04

The lines are the on-screen captions, so reading them aloud keeps voice and picture in sync. Read at a steady pace; each part's length is in brackets.

## Part 1 (0–10 s) · Isometric: Cold open
- Thousands of satellites are overhead right now.
- This camper has no cable, yet it is online.
- How? Let us take it apart.

## Part 2 (10–19 s) · Isometric: Why old satellite internet was slow
- Old satellites sit 36,000 kilometres up.
- Every request goes up and down, and so does the reply: about 600 milliseconds of lag.

## Part 3 (19–30 s) · Isometric: The big idea: fly low
- Starlink flies much lower: about 550 kilometres up.
- That is 65 times closer. But down low they move at 27,000 kilometres an hour, so one satellite is not enough.

## Part 4 (30–42 s) · Blueprint: Inside the satellite
- Each satellite is a flat panel, so dozens fit in one rocket.
- A solar array makes power. Antennas talk to your dish and to ground stations.
- Lasers link it to its neighbours. A thruster and star tracker keep it on course.

## Part 5 (42–51 s) · Blueprint: The clever flat dish
- At home, a flat dish packs tiny antennas.
- Fire them with tiny time offsets and the waves combine into a beam you can aim, instantly.

## Part 6 (51–60 s) · Blueprint: Following a moving target
- As satellites cross the sky, the dish hands you to the next one, again and again.

## Part 7 (60–72 s) · Map / travel: Getting up there: launches
- Rockets launch dozens at a time from Florida and California.
- The boosters land and fly again.
- Released low, the satellites climb to 550 kilometres on their own thrusters.

## Part 8 (72–81 s) · Map / travel: Thousands of orbits
- Their orbits weave around the planet in tilted rings.
- So wherever you stand, there is almost always one overhead.

## Part 9 (81–93 s) · Map / travel: The journey of one click
- Open a web page, and the request goes up to a satellite.
- Satellites relay it by laser to a ground station wired into the internet.
- The reply comes back the same way in tens of milliseconds.

## Part 10 (93–103 s) · Flat infographic: Why it feels fast
- Old satellite internet: about 600 milliseconds.
- Starlink: typically 25 to 60, close to many home connections.
- Enough for video calls and games.

## Part 11 (103–114 s) · Flat infographic: The catches
- The dish needs a clear view of the sky.
- Heavy rain or snow weakens the signal.
- Busy areas share limited capacity.
- And all those satellites worry astronomers.

## Part 12 (114–124 s) · Pastel sketch (Robo): Recap
- Fly low. Launch thousands. Steer the beam. Link to the ground.
- Subscribe, and tell me what to explain next.

## Fact notes for the narrator
- Rounded, typical figures: geostationary ≈ 36,000 km; Starlink ≈ 550 km; ≈ 27,000 km/h; old satellite lag ≈ 600 ms; Starlink typically 25–60 ms. Real results vary.
- Satellite parts (part 4) follow the common Starlink design and are simplified; details differ between versions.
- Launch sites, launch paths, orbit tracks and the click route are illustrative. The camper, the person and the Pacific route are examples.
- The cold open, the camper and the "Next up" teaser are creative additions.
