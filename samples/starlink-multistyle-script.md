# How Starlink Works (multi-style): voiceover script, 3:00

The lines are the on-screen captions, so reading them aloud keeps voice and picture in sync. Part 1 is a cold-open hook; its three lines start at 0 s, 5 s and 10 s. Every other part is 15 s with a line every 5 s (part 11 has four lines).

## Part 1 (0–15 s) · Isometric: Cold open
- Right now, thousands of satellites are racing over your head at 27,000 kilometres an hour.
- And this camper has no cable and no phone line, yet it is online.
- How? The trick is simpler than you think. Let us take it apart.

## Part 2 (15–30 s) · Isometric: Why old satellite internet was slow
- Satellite internet is not new, but it used to be painfully slow. Here is why.
- The satellites sit 36,000 kilometres up. Your request goes up and down, and so does the reply.
- That adds up to about 600 milliseconds of lag. Now watch what Starlink changes.

## Part 3 (30–45 s) · Isometric: The big idea: fly low
- Starlink's big idea? Fly much lower, only about 550 kilometres up.
- On the same scale, the old satellites are around 65 times higher. Shorter trip, faster reply.
- The catch: down low they race around at 27,000 kilometres an hour, so one satellite is never enough.

## Part 4 (45–60 s) · Blueprint: Inside the satellite
- Let us open one up. It is built as a flat panel, so dozens can stack in one rocket.
- A big solar array makes power. Phased-array antennas talk to your dish, and other antennas talk to ground stations.
- Lasers link it to its neighbours, an electric thruster keeps it in orbit, and a star tracker tells it where it points.

## Part 5 (60–75 s) · Blueprint: The clever flat dish
- At home, a flat dish packed with tiny antennas does the talking.
- Fire them with tiny time offsets, and the waves add up into a beam pointing where you want.
- Change the timing, and the beam re-aims in a fraction of a second.

## Part 6 (75–90 s) · Blueprint: Following a moving target
- Now the dish has a moving target: satellites cross the sky in minutes.
- It locks onto one, then hands you off to the next one coming up.
- This repeats again and again, and you never notice.

## Part 7 (90–105 s) · Map / travel: Getting up there: launches
- Satellites get there by rocket. Launches lift off from Florida and California, dozens at a time.
- The boosters land and fly again, which keeps launches cheaper.
- Released at a lower height, the satellites use their own thrusters to climb to about 550 kilometres.

## Part 8 (105–120 s) · Map / travel: Thousands of orbits
- Seen from above, their paths weave across the whole planet, in rings tilted at different angles.
- Add more rings and more satellites, and the whole world gets covered.
- Wherever you stand, their footprints overlap, so there is almost always one overhead.

## Part 9 (120–135 s) · Map / travel: The journey of one click
- So what happens when you open a web page? Your request goes up to a satellite.
- It can hop to another satellite by laser, then drop to a ground station wired into the regular internet.
- The answer makes the same trip back, in just tens of milliseconds.

## Part 10 (135–150 s) · Flat infographic: Why it feels fast
- So how much faster is it? Old satellite internet: roughly 600 milliseconds.
- Starlink: typically 25 to 60 milliseconds, not far from many home connections.
- That is enough for video calls and online games.

## Part 11 (150–165 s) · Flat infographic: The catches
- Now, it is not perfect. The dish needs a clear view of the sky.
- Heavy rain or snow can weaken the signal.
- Busy areas share limited capacity, so speeds can dip.
- And thousands of satellites worry astronomers and add to space junk.

## Part 12 (165–180 s) · Pastel sketch (Robo): Recap
- So: fly low for speed, launch thousands, steer the beam with timing, link to the ground.
- That is how a cabin in the middle of nowhere gets internet from the sky.
- If that made you curious, subscribe, and tell me what to explain next.

## Fact notes for the narrator
- Rounded, typical figures: geostationary ≈ 36,000 km; Starlink ≈ 550 km; ≈ 27,000 km/h; old satellite lag ≈ 600 ms; Starlink typically 25–60 ms; home fiber ≈ 10–20 ms. Real results vary.
- Satellite parts (part 4) follow the common Starlink design: solar array, user-link phased-array antennas, ground-link antennas, laser link, electric thruster, star tracker. Details differ between satellite versions; the drawing is simplified.
- Launch sites (Florida, California), launch paths, orbit tracks and the click route are illustrative, not exact. The camp, the person and the Pacific route are examples.
- The cold open, the camper and the "Next up" ideas are creative additions.
